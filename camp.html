Section 02

CAMP

A history. A sensibility. A queer survival strategy. And, as of May 2019, a theme — with all the political consequences that follow from institutionalization.

Susan Sontag, 1964 Queer Aesthetics Richard Dyer
Origins

Defining the Undefinable

Camp resists easy definition — and this resistance is part of its point. To camp something is to perform a kind of knowing, ironic excess that simultaneously takes its subject seriously and holds it at arm's length. Camp is artifice that knows it is artifice. It is theater that celebrates its own theatricality.

The word's origins are contested. The most widely cited etymology traces it to the French se camper — "to pose in an exaggerated fashion" — though some scholars trace it through British gay slang of the late nineteenth century, where it appeared in the Polari lexicon used by gay men to communicate covertly in public. In either case, the term carries from its earliest uses a connotation of performed, deliberate excess.

Before Susan Sontag codified it in academic language, camp was a sensibility understood and practiced by communities who had no formal name for it. It circulated in theatrical subcultures, among female impersonators, in the underground gay nightlife of early twentieth-century New York, London, and Paris. It was a way of surviving a world that did not make room for you, by transforming that world into theater, and yourself into its star.

"Camp is a certain mode of aestheticism. It is one way of seeing the world as an aesthetic phenomenon." — Susan Sontag, "Notes on Camp" (1964)

Key Characteristics of Camp
  • Theatricality — life experienced as performance
  • Artifice — the constructed over the natural
  • Excess — more is always more
  • Irony — knowing, self-aware distance from content
  • Failed seriousness — the tragic rendered comic
  • Androgyny — the destabilization of gender binaries
  • Detachment — engagement without full investment
Queer Origins

Camp as Queer Survival

For queer communities — particularly gay men — camp has functioned not merely as an aesthetic preference but as a survival strategy: a coded language for navigating hostile public space, a mode of solidarity that operated below the radar of heteronormative culture.

Richard Dyer's The Culture of Queers (2002) offers the most sustained theoretical account of camp as a distinctively queer cultural practice. For Dyer, camp is inseparable from the experience of being queer in a world that systematically denies, pathologizes, and criminalizes queer existence. Camp's irony is not mere playfulness — it is a response to the impossibility of taking that world at face value. If the mainstream insists on naturalizing heterosexuality, domesticity, and gender normativity, camp responds by making everything theatrical, artificial, and slightly ridiculous.

"Camp is a way of being that refuses the authority of the dominant culture's definitions of the real." — Richard Dyer, The Culture of Queers (2002)

Camp's relationship to drag is foundational. Drag — the performance of gender as costume, the hyper-stylized impersonation of femininity by gay men — is one of the most concentrated expressions of camp as politics. When a drag queen performs the Woman, she simultaneously celebrates and subverts the category, revealing it as a performance, a role, an artifice that anyone can play. The political charge of this gesture depends on its marginality: drag's subversion of gender norms derives its force from the fact that those norms are enforced with violence.

Dyer also emphasizes the collective dimension of camp. Camp is not simply an individual sensibility — it is a community practice, a shared aesthetic that creates bonds of recognition among queer people. The ability to read camp — to "get" the joke, to recognize the irony, to appreciate the excess — marks one as part of the community. This is what Sontag, an outsider to the queer subculture she was describing, somewhat missed: camp is not just a way of seeing, but a way of belonging.

Greteman and Stiegler (2019) extend this point across generations: queer communities sustain themselves in part through visual culture, developing aesthetic vocabularies that carry queer history and collective identity in ways that resist easy verbal articulation. Camp's visual languages — its codes, its excesses, its knowing performances — function as forms of intergenerational inheritance. What is at stake when those languages are absorbed into mainstream fashion discourse is not just aesthetic meaning but queer community memory.

Blackman's (2014) framework of subculture theory is directly applicable here. For Blackman, subcultures are constituted by their deviation from dominant norms — by their deliberate cultivation of difference. Camp, in its queer subcultural context, performs exactly this function: it marks queer communities as different, communicates solidarity among members, and converts the experience of marginalization into a source of cultural power and creativity.

Blackman (2014) on Subculture

Blackman argues that subculture theory, from the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS) onward, has consistently identified style as the primary medium through which subcultural resistance is articulated. Camp's style — its visual excess, its theatrical self-presentation — functions precisely in this way as a visible, legible mark of subcultural membership and resistance.

Crucially, Blackman notes that subcultural meaning is inherently relational — it depends on its difference from the mainstream. When that difference collapses, so does the subcultural charge.

History

Camp Through the Decades

Late 19th C.
Polari & Aesthetic Movement

Camp circulates in British gay subcultures through Polari, a coded language used by gay men, sailors, and theatrical communities. Oscar Wilde's aestheticism — "art for art's sake," the cultivation of beauty as life's supreme value — anticipates camp's love of artifice. Wilde's trials (1895) mark a moment of catastrophic mainstream visibility for queer culture.

1920s–1940s
Drag Balls & Underground Queer Culture

The Harlem drag balls of the 1920s create some of the most sustained camp communities in American history — spaces where gay men, transgender women, and gender-nonconforming people performed theatricalized gender for appreciative queer audiences. Camp circulates in underground theatrical and film circles; female impersonation thrives in queer nightlife.

1964
Susan Sontag, "Notes on Camp"

Sontag's essay in Partisan Review introduces the concept of camp to mainstream intellectual discourse. It is one of the most influential essays in American cultural criticism. Sontag's achievement — and her limitation — is to extract camp from its queer origins and describe it as a "sensibility" available to any aesthete. Many queer critics have subsequently argued that this extraction was itself an act of cultural appropriation.

1969–1970s
Stonewall & Queer Liberation

The Stonewall riots (1969) — led substantially by drag queens and trans women of color — mark a politicization of queer camp. Camp excess becomes explicitly political: queer communities reclaim the right to public visibility and self-expression. Drag culture becomes increasingly central to queer organizing and identity.

1980s–1990s
Pop Camp & ACT UP

Camp enters popular culture through pop stars (Madonna, Boy George, Divine, Klaus Nomi) while simultaneously intensifying as a political mode during the AIDS crisis. ACT UP's theatrical, campy protest aesthetic — die-ins, drag spectacles, confrontational performance — demonstrates camp's enduring power as political form. Meanwhile, John Waters brings camp to mainstream cinema.

2009–Present
RuPaul's Drag Race & Digital Camp

RuPaul's Drag Race transforms drag and camp from subcultural to mainstream entertainment. The show creates a new vocabulary — "throwing shade," "reading," "serving looks" — that circulates widely in mainstream digital culture, substantially separated from its queer origins. Camp becomes legible to general audiences, setting the stage for the 2019 Met Gala's institutional embrace.

May 6, 2019
The Met Gala Threshold

The Metropolitan Museum of Art themes its annual Costume Institute Gala "Camp: Notes on Fashion." For the first time, camp is institutionally endorsed, curated, and broadcast to a mass global audience. The question this project addresses: what happens to subcultural meaning at this threshold?

Critical Analysis

The Politics of Mainstreaming Camp

The 2019 Met Gala's choice of "Camp" as its theme was met with both celebration and critique. The celebration centered on visibility: camp (and by implication, queer culture) was being honored at one of fashion's most prestigious stages.

The critique centered on extraction. Camp's subcultural meaning, many queer critics argued, depends on its context: it means something different when performed by a Black gay man who lived through the AIDS crisis (Billy Porter) than when replicated as a trend by a fast fashion brand targeting teenage consumers. The Met Gala, with its $35,000-a-plate exclusivity and its global media reach, was not democratizing camp — it was aestheticizing it, stripping it of its political charge and repackaging it as visual spectacle.

Drag is not a costume. Camp is not an aesthetic. They are survival languages — ways of living through the unbearable by making it beautiful and ridiculous at the same time.

Blackman's (2014) analysis of subculture theory offers a structural account of this tension. He argues that the mainstream's appropriation of subcultural style is a recurrent historical phenomenon — one that inevitably transforms the meaning of the appropriated style. When punk's safety pins appeared on fashion runways, they ceased to signify working-class rage and became signs of fashionable rebellion. When camp's theatricality appears on the Met Gala red carpet and then in Zara's new arrivals section, it ceases to signify queer survival and becomes a sign of aesthetic adventurousness.

Adorno and Rabinbach (1975) give this process its structural logic: the culture industry does not simply borrow from resistant cultural forms — it processes them, producing standardized versions that are legible and consumable at scale. The original's critical charge is not merely diluted but systematically neutralized, replaced by a surface resemblance that satisfies demand while foreclosing the subversive content that gave the original its meaning.

This does not mean the Met Gala was simply an act of erasure. The gala also created genuine visibility: Billy Porter's arrival being carried on a litter in full camp regalia was watched by millions who had never seen such an image in mainstream media. Representation at this scale has real effects, including for queer young people seeing themselves reflected in mass media for the first time.

The question is not whether mainstreaming camp was entirely good or entirely bad — it is what kind of transformation occurred, at what speed, and with what consequences for the communities from which camp derives its meaning. That is the question the data in the next section addresses.

The Democratization vs. Dilution Debate
Democratization

Camp aesthetics reach wider audiences; queer cultural forms gain mainstream recognition; visibility creates solidarity; younger audiences exposed to queer history through fashion.

Dilution

Subcultural meaning stripped from aesthetic markers; fast fashion commodifies queer style without queer community benefit; irony collapsed into "extra"; political charge neutralized.

Clarification

What Camp Is Not

One of the key analytical questions this project addresses: how did social media discourse after the 2019 Met Gala define camp? The textual analysis (see Data & Analysis) reveals that mainstream social media largely collapsed camp into a set of simpler aesthetic categories.

Camp IS NOT Simply "Extra"

The reduction of camp to "extra" — meaning excessive, over-the-top, dramatic — strips it of its knowing irony. Something "extra" can be unintentionally excessive. Camp requires self-awareness: the performer knows they are performing, and so does the audience. The joke requires everyone to be in on it.

Camp IS NOT Simply Glamour

Glamour seeks to appear effortlessly beautiful and naturally superior. Camp celebrates the effort, the construction, the artifice — it makes the seams visible on purpose. Camp is anti-glamour in this sense: it loves the ostentatious, the labored, the theatrical over the seemingly natural.

Camp IS NOT Simply Drag

Drag is one of camp's most powerful expressions, but camp is not reducible to drag. Camp can operate through fashion, film, interior design, speech, humor. What unifies these is the attitude — ironic, theatrical, excessive — not the form. Reducing camp to drag also erases camp's non-drag manifestations in queer culture.
