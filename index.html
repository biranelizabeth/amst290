Core Argument

The 2019 Met Gala Didn't Just Display Camp. It Exported It.

When the Metropolitan Museum of Art themed its 2019 Costume Institute Gala around Susan Sontag's "Notes on Camp," it set in motion a cultural mechanism: the conversion of a historically queer, subcultural mode of being into a mass aesthetic commodity. This project uses hashtag network data, computer vision, and natural language processing to trace that conversion across social media in real time, asking what happens to a subculture when its aesthetics go viral.

Research Questions

What Are We Asking?

01

Did the Met Gala's "Camp" theme democratize or dilute camp's subcultural origins when it entered mainstream social media discourse?

02

How did fast fashion brands respond to the Met Gala camp moment — and how quickly did runway-to-retail aesthetic translation occur?

Why This Matters

The Stakes of Subcultural Diffusion

Camp has functioned for decades as a queer survival strategy — a mode of communicating identity, solidarity, and resistance through style, irony, and theatrical excess. Its origins lie in the margins: drag bars, underground performance circuits, and gay subcultures that predated legal recognition.

"Camp is the answer to the problem of how to be a dandy in an age of mass culture." — Susan Sontag, "Notes on Camp" (1964)

When an institution as powerful as the Met, presided over by Vogue editor-in-chief Anna Wintour and co-chaired by Lady Gaga, Harry Styles, Serena Williams, and Alessandro Michele, chooses camp as its central theme, it performs an act of translation: from subculture to spectacle, from resistance to red carpet, from the margin to the mainstream.

Sociologist Sean Blackman's work on subculture theory reminds us that subcultures derive their meaning precisely from their relationship to, and distance from, the dominant culture. As Blackman (2014) argues, subcultural identity is constituted through deviance: through codes, styles, and practices that mark insiders as different, resistant, and deliberately other.

Richard Dyer (2002), writing on queer cultural production, identifies camp as one of the primary aesthetic languages through which gay and queer communities have historically articulated their experience of being "other" in a heteronormative world. Camp's excess, its theatricality, its knowingness — these are not mere stylistic flourishes but political acts of self-definition.

Adorno and Rabinbach's (1975) culture industry thesis gives this process a structural name: the culture industry converts authentic cultural production into standardized commodities, rendering what was once particular and resistant legible to the widest possible audience. What emerges from that process is recognizable but hollowed — the surface features of the original, minus its critical content.

The moment a subculture's markers become the mainstream's aesthetic playground, those markers lose their constitutive charge. Instagram replication is not the same as queer solidarity. This project asks: what happens to camp when it becomes trending?

Methodology

Three Analytical Lenses

01

Hashtag & Network Analysis

Mapping the spread of #MetGala2019, #Camp, #CampFashion, and related hashtags across Instagram and Twitter/X using archived data. Network graphs reveal how content clusters formed around distinct communities — fashion bloggers, drag culture, luxury brands, and fast fashion — and how meaning shifted between them.

Tools: Gephi, Palladio, Twitter Academic API archive

02

Visual & Image Analysis

Computer vision applied to a corpus of tagged posts to identify dominant colors, composition patterns, and detected clothing objects — tracing the visual signatures of camp across the spread: maximalism, excessive ornamentation, theatricality. Were camp's visual codes preserved or diluted?

Tools: Google Vision API, pre-trained ResNet models, ImageNet

03

Textual / Caption Analysis

NLP tools applied to post captions reveal whether camp was discussed in its subcultural terms — irony, theatricality, queer history — or reduced to simpler aesthetic labels: "extra," "glam," "over the top." Topic modeling traces the semantic drift from subcultural discourse to consumer language.

Tools: NLTK, spaCy, VADER sentiment, LDA topic modeling
